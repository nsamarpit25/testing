# Ep 4 facts (checked 2026-10-24)

Primary source for everything on screen or in the voice: US Bureau of Reclamation (usbr.gov).

1. **125 years to cool in one pour; it would have cracked.** "Bureau of Reclamation engineers calculated that if the
   dam were built in a single continuous pour, the concrete would have gotten so hot that it would have taken 125
   years for the concrete to cool to ambient temperatures. The resulting stresses would have caused the dam to crack
   and crumble away." Heat came from the curing (setting) concrete.
   https://www.usbr.gov/lc/hooverdam/History/essays/concrete.html
   (Some secondary sites say 150 years; not used.)
2. **Why it cracks: hot core vs cooling surface** (line 2, general engineering, not Hoover-specific). Cement
   hydration heats mass concrete; the surface loses heat faster than the core, and the temperature difference and
   later contraction create tensile stresses that crack it. Standard mass-concrete practice (ACI):
   https://www.concrete.org/publications/internationalconcreteabstractsportal/m/details/id/13475
   USBR only says "the resulting stresses would have caused the dam to crack", so the script keeps it simple.
3. **Built in separate columns.** "Rather than being a single block of concrete, the dam was built as a series of
   individual columns. Trapezoidal in shape, the columns rose in five foot lifts." Blocks "varied in size from about
   60 feet square at the upstream face of the dam to about 25 feet square at the downstream face"; an estimated 215
   blocks (columns); no more than 5 ft per block per 72 hours. Faces had vertical interlocking grooves (keys).
   https://www.usbr.gov/lc/hooverdam/History/essays/concrete.html
   https://www.usbr.gov/lc/hooverdam/faqs/damfaqs.html
4. **Over 582 miles of 1-inch steel pipe.** "By embedding more than 582 miles of 1-inch steel pipe in the concrete and
   circulating ice water through it from a refrigeration plant [that] could produce 1,000 tons of ice in 24 hours."
   Each form contained "cooling coils of 1-inch thin-walled steel pipe".
   https://www.usbr.gov/lc/hooverdam/faqs/damfaqs.html
5. **River water first, then chilled water from a refrigeration plant.** "When the concrete was first poured, river
   water was circulated through these pipes. Once the concrete had received a first initial cooling, chilled water
   from a refrigeration plant on the lower cofferdam was circulated through the coils to finish the cooling."
   Plant capacity: 1,000 tons of ice per 24 h (FAQ). Script says "giant refrigeration plant" (not the ice number,
   to save words).
   https://www.usbr.gov/lc/hooverdam/History/essays/concrete.html
6. **Columns shrink apart, grout fills gaps and pipes, one monolith.** "After the concrete was cooled, a cement and
   water mixture called grout was forced into the spaces created between the columns by the contraction of the
   cooled concrete to form a monolithic (one-piece) structure." Pipes "were cut off and pressure grouted at 300
   pounds per square inch".
   https://www.usbr.gov/lc/hooverdam/faqs/damfaqs.html
   https://www.usbr.gov/lc/hooverdam/History/essays/concrete.html
7. **Cooled in under two years.** First concrete placed June 6, 1933; "Cooling was completed in March 1935" (about
   21 months). Derived from the two USBR FAQ dates. (Last concrete went in May 29, 1935, so don't claim cooling
   finished "before the last pour".)
   https://www.usbr.gov/lc/hooverdam/faqs/damfaqs.html

Context, not in the voice: dam 726.4 ft high; 3.25 million cubic yards in the dam (4.36 million incl. powerplant
and works); ~160,000 cubic yards placed per month (FAQ). The essay adds that the curing heat could have baked
500,000 loaves of bread a day for three years.
